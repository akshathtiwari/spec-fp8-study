\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Speculative decoding and FP8 quantization are both standard in production
LLM serving, and their interaction is almost always reported from single
runs. We set out to measure that interaction on Ada-class hardware and
could not, four times, until the measurement apparatus itself was fixed.

We identify and quantify four confounds on vLLM 0.29.0 / NVIDIA L4 (SM89).
First, attention-backend selection is \emph{conditioned on KV-cache dtype},
so an FP8-versus-BF16 comparison under default settings silently swaps the
attention kernel as well; target and draft models select backends
independently, so pinning one does not pin the other. Second, speculative
throughput is \emph{unreproducible across boots} of an identical
configuration while acceptance is not: coefficient of variation 13.92\%
over 12 fixed-prompt boots. The dispersion was present with CUDA graphs
enabled and collapsed under \tw{-{}-enforce-eager} (CV 1.44\%); the
responsible mechanism remains unidentified. Third, acceptance
length $\tau$ has a boot-to-boot \emph{coefficient of variation of 0.62\%}
under repeated identical boots, which we could not find reported; our own
FP8-precision comparison is unreplicated and we withdraw the equivalence
reading we previously gave it.
Fourth, the metric commonly called \emph{inter-token latency is, when
computed from stream-arrival timestamps, inter-chunk latency}: a speculative step emits every accepted token in one
stream chunk, so the measured gap exceeds the amortised production time
per emitted token by roughly~$\tau$.

The first three add error; the fourth reverses the ranking. Under a standard
p95-ITL service objective, every speculative configuration we measured
scores \emph{zero goodput at concurrency 64 while producing the most tokens
per second in the grid}. We report FP8~$\times$~speculative results with all
four controlled, and release the harness, the raw records, and the full
history of our own corrections --- preserved analysis revisions, including
two that withdrew headline claims of this paper, each traceable to the same
error shape.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Speculative decoding trades a small draft model's cheap guesses against the
target model's expensive verification~\cite{leviathan2023,chen2023}. Its
benefit is governed almost entirely by one quantity: $\tau$, the mean number
of tokens emitted per verification step (accepted draft tokens plus the
bonus token). We call this \emph{acceptance length} rather than an
acceptance \emph{rate}, since $\tau > 1$ by construction and is not a
proportion. FP8 quantization independently
reduces weight and KV-cache footprint, which matters because KV capacity is
what binds under concurrency~\cite{kwon2023}. Serving stacks ship both. The
obvious question --- does quantization degrade acceptance, and what does the
combination buy under load --- is asked frequently and answered from
single-run measurements.

We could not answer it. Four separate times, a result we were about to
report turned out to be produced by something other than the variable under
study. This paper reports those four things, because they are larger than
the effect they obscured.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Backend substitution under dtype change} (\S\ref{sec:c1}).
vLLM's \tw{auto} backend selection is conditioned on KV-cache dtype, and
target and draft select independently. A precision A/B under defaults is a
kernel A/B as well.
\item \textbf{Unreproducible speculative throughput} (\S\ref{sec:c2}).
Identical configurations have CV 13.92\% across 12 fixed-prompt boots at
constant acceptance. We localise this to \emph{draft-model} speculation:
n-gram shows CV 2.67\%. We do not identify the mechanism. \tw{-{}-enforce-eager} removes the
variance but is not a free control --- it costs FP8-weight configurations
a fifth to a half of their throughput and cannot boot speculative FP8-KV
cells at all on this card.
\item \textbf{Acceptance dispersion} (\S\ref{sec:c3}). $\tau$ has CV
0.62\% across 12 fixed-prompt boots. We withdraw two claims of our own that
rested on one boot per arm, and decline to convert the dispersion into an
equivalence bound for a comparison measured at a different operating
point.
\item \textbf{ITL is inter-chunk latency} (\S\ref{sec:c4}). A speculative
step emits every accepted token in one stream chunk, so a p95-ITL objective
ranks the highest-throughput configuration in the grid as fully
non-compliant. This one does not add error --- it inverts the decision.
\item \textbf{Measurements after diagnosing four confounds}
(\S\ref{sec:results}): compatibility, KV capacity, task accuracy at
$n{=}256$, and goodput. Three of the four are controlled; draft-side
backend selection is not, because the engine offers no way to pin it.
\item \textbf{A fully auditable artifact} (\S\ref{sec:artifact}):
append-only raw records, script-regenerated derived tables, provenance
checked by a script that fails the build, and every retraction preserved
rather than removed.
\end{enumerate}

We emphasise the last because it is what made the others findable. Most of
our corrections were caught by re-reading stored records against a
hypothesis formed later --- impossible if results are summarised at write
time.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Engine & vLLM 0.29.0, pinned; version read from the running server over HTTP\\
GPU & NVIDIA L4 (SM89, Ada, 24\,GB nominal / 22.03\,GiB usable), rented per second\\
Target & Qwen3-4B\\
Draft & \tw{z-lab/Qwen3-4B-DFlash-b16}, block size 16, 5 speculative tokens\\
Also & n-gram (prompt-lookup) speculation, no draft checkpoint\\
Workload & GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021}, sampled without replacement\\
Base image & \tw{nvidia/cuda:13.0.3-devel-ubuntu24.04}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
L4 was chosen deliberately: it is the compute capability named in the
upstream reports that motivated the study, and at 22\,GiB the KV-capacity
constraint actually binds, which it does not on an 80\,GB card.

\paragraph{The harness never imports the engine.} It launches vLLM as a
subprocess and speaks HTTP. This costs some convenience and buys the
guarantee that what we measure is what a serving deployment runs, not an
in-process configuration no server would produce.

\paragraph{Every measurement is content-addressed.} A configuration hashes
to a \tw{cell\_id}; identical configuration yields an identical id on any
machine. Records are append-only, so a re-run supersedes rather than
overwrites and the superseded record remains readable. Several of our
corrections depend on that history being intact.

\paragraph{Definition.} We define
\[
\tau \;=\; 1 + \frac{\text{accepted draft tokens}}{\text{verification steps}}.
\]
The $+1$ is the bonus token, which vLLM's counter excludes. Omitting it
understates $\tau$ by exactly 1 and silently changes every speedup ratio
derived from it.

\section{Confound 1: the attention backend moves when the dtype moves}\label{sec:c1}

vLLM's \tw{auto} attention-backend selection is a function of KV-cache
dtype. At BF16 KV it selects FlashAttention-2; at \tw{fp8\_e4m3} it selects
FlashInfer. An experimenter varying only precision therefore varies the
attention kernel too, and any resulting difference is jointly attributable.

It is worse than one substitution. Target and draft models select backends
\emph{independently}, at two distinct call sites, so pinning the backend
affects the target while the draft continues to select on its own. A
pinned-backend precision comparison is still confounded on the draft side
for speculative cells, which we record per cell rather than assert away.

Compatibility is not uniform. Of the four attention backends vLLM 0.29.0
offers on SM89, FP8 KV runs on exactly two.

\tw{launch\_failed} is rejection at configuration time.}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
Backend & FP8 KV status & KV tokens\\
\midrule
FLASHINFER & ok & 115{,}920\\
TRITON\_ATTN & ok & 129{,}584\\
FLASH\_ATTN & launch\_failed & ---\\
FLEX\_ATTENTION & launch\_failed & ---\\
\tw{auto} & ok (selects FLASHINFER) & 115{,}920\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
This is the mechanism behind the dtype-conditioned selection: \tw{auto} does
not switch away from FlashAttention-2 under FP8 KV as a preference, it
switches because FlashAttention-2 \emph{cannot} serve FP8 KV on this
hardware. The original hypothesis motivating this study --- that FlashInfer's
FP8 attention kernels are SM90-only, so FP8 KV plus non-causal drafting
would crash on Ada --- is refuted. The combination runs.

\paragraph{Our own error here.} We initially concluded that the backend
selection flag was accepted, echoed back, validated against, and then
ignored, and reported this upstream. That was wrong: the flag controls the
target, and what we observed was the draft selecting independently. We
corrected the report. The mistake is instructive because we compared
requested-versus-selected backend for \emph{a server} when there is no such
thing as a server's backend --- there are two.

\section{Confound 2: speculative throughput is unreproducible across boots}\label{sec:c2}

Booting an identical speculative configuration repeatedly produces widely
scattered throughput while acceptance stays put. Over 12 boots that replay
one fixed prompt set, the coefficient of variation is \textbf{13.92\%}
(median 40.8 tok/s).

Two measurement choices matter here, and we got both wrong in earlier
revisions. First, we report CV rather than a max/min range: a range grows
with sample size by construction, so ranges from arms with different boot
counts cannot be compared. Second, we restrict to \emph{fixed-prompt}
boots. Pooling in grid records, whose repeats draw different prompts by
design, mixes prompt variation into what is meant to be boot variation and
inflated this figure to 29.92\%. The pooled number is reported separately in
the artifact and is not a boot-dispersion estimate.

Pooling every valid default-arm boot at concurrency 1 (n = 23):
\[
\begin{aligned}
&24.8\;\; 28.4\;\; 29.5\;\; 31.9\;\; 32.1\;\; 33.2\;\; 37.0\;\; 39.1\;\; 40.6\;\; 41.1\\
&41.3\;\; 43.7\;\; 44.2\;\; 44.5\;\; 45.5\;\; 47.3\;\; 50.7\;\; 60.8\;\; 61.2\;\; 62.0\;\; 64.7\;\; 66.5\;\; 73.6
\end{aligned}
\]

\textbf{We previously reported this as bimodal and retract that.} An
earlier draft claimed two discrete modes, on the basis of five boots with a
gap in the middle. At n = 23 the gap is filled: fitting one versus two
Gaussian components gives BIC 190.50 against 192.10, a $\Delta$BIC of
$-1.60$ \emph{favouring a single component}. The retraction is recorded in
the artifact. It is the same error we document elsewhere in this paper —
reading structure off a sample too small to support it — and we made it in
our own headline.

\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/bimodality.pdf}
\caption{One dot per boot of an identical configuration at concurrency 1.
DFlash's default arm is a wide, continuously filled distribution; its eager
arm and both n-gram arms are tight. Note that every eager boot exceeds every
one of the 23 default boots. An earlier version of this figure showed five
default boots and was captioned as two clusters; plotting all of them is
what refuted that.}
\label{fig:bimodality}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The dispersion is associated with the CUDA-graph path; we do
not identify the mechanism.} vLLM emits, at every speculative boot, a
warning that \tw{CUDAGraphMode.FULL\_AND\_PIECEWISE} is unsupported with
spec-decode for the FlashInfer backend, and falls back. Pinning
\tw{cudagraph\_mode: PIECEWISE} lands at the low end (30.04 versus a 30.57
default mean). Disabling CUDA graphs collapses the dispersion to CV 1.44\%
over 8 boots.

That is localisation, not causation, and we are explicit about the gap.
\tw{-{}-enforce-eager} changes the entire execution path: graph capture,
memory reservation, boot feasibility (\S\ref{sec:results}) and FP8 kernel
performance all move with it. We did not separate boot state from host
state --- rented L4 instances differ in physical GPU, clock and power
behaviour between runs, and we did not record GPU identity. The defensible
claim is that \emph{the dispersion appeared in graph-enabled DFlash runs and
collapsed under eager execution, and which graph, scheduler, allocator,
kernel or host property is responsible remains unknown.}

\paragraph{The sharpest instance.} One content-addressed cell
(\tw{dflash\,|\,bf16\,|\,fp8\_e4m3\,|\,FLASHINFER}, graphs on) was measured
in two ordinary grid runs:

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Session & measurements (tok/s) & mean & $\tau$\\
\midrule
grid 1 & 28.59,\; 29.39,\; 33.81 & 30.60 & 4.183\\
grid 2 & 64.89,\; 71.77 & 68.33 & 4.211\\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{ratio} & \textbf{2.23$\times$} & 0.68\% apart\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
Same \tw{cell\_id}, therefore the same configuration by construction, and a
matching \tw{argv} fingerprint. The $\tau$ difference of 0.68\% is
within its measured dispersion (\S\ref{sec:c3}). Every earlier
demonstration of this effect compared \emph{something} --- different arms,
different cells, boots inside a test built to show it. This compares nothing.

\paragraph{We report no speedup ratio from this effect.} Measuring both arms
inside a single run, with the default boot landing high in its range, gives
$1.13\times / 1.06\times / 1.05\times$ at concurrency 1/16/64. Against a
low-end boot the same eager numbers give $2.19\times$. The ratio is a
statement about where in the default arm's distribution the comparison boot
happened to land, not about the flag.

What is stable is the \emph{dispersion}: CV 13.92\% over 12 default boots
against 1.44\% over 8 eager boots, and every eager boot exceeds every
default boot. Turning CUDA graphs off does not reliably make speculative
decoding faster by a stated factor --- it makes it \emph{reproducible}, and
whatever throughput it gains is however far down the distribution the
default would otherwise have fallen.

Our first write-up of this section led with $2.38\times$, obtained by
comparing against an arm we assumed representative, which was one draw from
the wide distribution this same section describes.

\paragraph{It is specific to draft-model speculation.} Running the identical
protocol with n-gram (prompt-lookup) speculation finds no such dispersion
across twelve boots --- CV 2.67\% against DFlash's 13.92\% --- and no
benefit from \tw{-{}-enforce-eager}. These are the same
fact arriving twice: if throughput is already reproducible, there is nothing
for the flag to stabilise. As a measurement sanity check, n-gram's acceptance is deterministic
($\tau = 1.732$ on every boot, spread $1.000\times$) where DFlash's moves
4.09--4.43, which is what a prompt-matching mechanism should do.

Twelve boots cannot prove absence; they bound it. At matched $n=12$,
DFlash gives CV 13.92\% and n-gram 2.67\%, a factor of five apart, so if
n-gram shared DFlash's dispersion, drawing twelve boots that tight would be
unlikely. We report this as a localisation, not an
exclusion. The
consequence is that a claim like ours has to name the mechanism:
``speculative throughput is unreproducible'' would be refuted by the first
reader who tries prompt-lookup.

\section{Confound 3: acceptance dispersion is small, and had not been
measured}\label{sec:c3}

Across 12 boots of an identical configuration with identical prompts,
acceptance has a coefficient of variation of \textbf{0.62\%} (mean $\tau$
4.1055, sd 0.0256, 95\% CI on the mean [4.0893, 4.1218]).

\paragraph{We previously called this a ``noise floor'' and withdraw that.}
An earlier revision reported the \emph{range} of four boots, 1.32\%, and
described it as a floor below which effects were ``unfalsifiable''. That was
wrong in three ways, and we state them because the error is instructive.

A range is not a dispersion statistic that can be compared or reused: it
grows with sample size by construction. Measured again at $n = 12$, the same
quantity's range is \textbf{1.38\%} --- it increased, which is what ranges
do and what floors do not. Second, a detection threshold depends on
variance, sample size, power and the chosen test, none of which a range
encodes; effects below it are not unfalsifiable, they are undetected at that
$n$. Third, we transplanted a figure measured at $\tau \approx 4.1$ on
GSM8K at 768 tokens onto an earlier result measured at $\tau \approx 2.71$
on a different prompt set at 256 tokens.

\paragraph{What the data supports, and what it does not.} It is tempting
to convert 0.62\% into a detection threshold and declare the 0.7\% FP8
precision effect ``not detected''. We decline, for a reason that is the
whole point of this section.

The 12 replicated boots are DFlash, GSM8K, 768-token budget, at
$\tau \approx 4.1$. The 0.7\% precision effect comes from \emph{single-boot}
cells at $\tau \approx 2.71$ on a different prompt set at a 256-token
budget. There are not twelve boots per precision arm; there is one. Using
dispersion measured at one operating point as an uncertainty bound for a
comparison made at another is precisely the transplant we criticise two
paragraphs above, and an earlier revision of this paper did it anyway.

The defensible statement is therefore weaker and shorter: \emph{our FP8
precision comparison is unreplicated, and cannot establish either a
difference or an equivalence.} Establishing equivalence would need repeated
boots per precision arm at a declared margin, which we did not run.

\paragraph{Acceptance is not continuously noisy.} Across those 12 boots
$\tau$ takes exactly \textbf{two distinct values}, 4.0914 (nine boots) and
4.1479 (three). Given fixed prompts, acceptance is near-deterministic with
occasional discrete shifts, so modelling it as Gaussian measurement noise
was the wrong frame independently of the sample-size error.

\paragraph{What survives.} We withdraw a previously-published claim of ours
that backend choice perturbs $\tau$ by 2.25\%. That figure came from
\emph{one boot per backend}, and a single boot is not a basis for a timing
claim on this stack (\S\ref{sec:c2}). It is withdrawn because it is
unreplicated, which needs no appeal to a floor.

\paragraph{What we would claim, narrowly.} We could not find a published
dispersion estimate for $\tau$ under repeated identical boots, and effects
smaller than the one we measure here have been reported as real elsewhere.
Kaplan~\cite{kaplan2026calibrated}, concurrently, calibrates an LLM judge by
scoring ``two ordinary runs of a model given the same prompts'' and adds a
null condition ``provably identical in distribution\ldots whose measured
difference must be zero''. That is the same epistemic move, applied to
output quality; independent concurrent arrival at it is evidence the
practice is right. Our contribution is to apply it to acceptance, and to
report that the dispersion is small rather than to assert a threshold.

\paragraph{Operating point matters.} Our 0.62\% is measured at
$\tau \approx 4.1$, GSM8K, 768-token budget. The same configuration at a
256-token budget on a different prompt set gave $\tau \approx 2.71$ --- a
55\% difference from generation length and prompt distribution alone. A
$\tau$ quoted without both is close to uninterpretable, and the literature
quotes $\tau$ freely.

\section{Confound 4: ``inter-token latency'' is inter-chunk latency}\label{sec:c4}

The first three confounds add error. This one reverses the ranking.

A streaming client measures the gap between \emph{arrivals}. vLLM emits one
SSE chunk per decoding step, and a speculative verification step emits every
accepted draft token at once. So a metric computed from stream-arrival
timestamps --- which is how our own harness computed it, and the natural
reading of a streaming API --- is \emph{inter-chunk} latency, and on a
speculative server it exceeds the amortised production time per emitted
token by roughly $\tau$.

We make no claim about which specific load generators do this. We did not
audit any, and saying ``every load generator'' without naming and versioning
them would be the kind of unsupported universal this paper objects to. What
we establish is the mechanism and that one harness --- ours --- fell into
it.

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\caption{Concurrency 64, CUDA graphs on, FP8 weights, auto KV, identical
prompts and SLO.}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
& measured gap & tokens/chunk & amortised per token & throughput\\
\midrule
speculative (DFlash) & \textbf{93.8\,ms} & \textbf{4.122} & \textbf{22.8\,ms} & \textbf{2219 tok/s}\\
non-speculative & 33.8\,ms & 1.007 & 33.6\,ms & 1281 tok/s\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
The 22.8\,ms figure is \textbf{amortised production time per emitted token},
not a per-token latency: those four tokens become visible to the client
together, at one instant, 93.8\,ms apart. Speculation produces tokens
$1.5\times$ faster on that amortised basis and appears $2.8\times$ slower on
the recorded metric. The \tw{tokens\_per\_chunk} of 4.122 tracks the
measured $\tau$ of ${\approx}4.2$, as it must.

\caption{Goodput at p95 ITL $\le$ 50\,ms, TTFT $\le$ 1000\,ms, concurrency 64.}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule

\midrule
dflash\,|\,fp8\,|\,auto & \textbf{8.31} & \textbf{0\%} & \textbf{0.000}\\
dflash\,|\,bf16\,|\,auto & 6.13 & 0\% & 0.000\\
none\,|\,fp8\,|\,auto & 5.20 & 100\% & \textbf{5.188}\\
none\,|\,bf16\,|\,auto & 3.37 & 6\% & 0.209\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
Under this objective every speculative configuration receives zero compliant
goodput while producing the most tokens per second in the grid.

\paragraph{This is not an artifact of the 50\,ms threshold.} Sweeping the
ITL target at TTFT $\le$ 1000\,ms:

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
conc & mech & 20\,ms & 35\,ms & 50\,ms & 75\,ms\\
\midrule
1 & dflash & 0\% & 64\% & 100\% & 100\%\\
1 & none & 0\% & 89\% & 99\% & 100\%\\
16 & dflash & 0\% & 0\% & 74\% & 99\%\\
16 & none & 0\% & 89\% & 98\% & 100\%\\
64 & \textbf{dflash} & \textbf{0\%} & \textbf{0\%} & \textbf{0\%} & \textbf{0\%}\\
64 & none & 0\% & 3\% & 99\% & 100\%\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
At concurrency 64 the speculative configuration scores zero at every
threshold we tested, while the non-speculative one reaches 99\% at 50\,ms.
The mechanism is not a coincidence of threshold choice: the chunk cadence at
that concurrency is 93.8\,ms, so \emph{any} inter-arrival target below the
cadence fails by construction, and any target above it would pass
trivially. At concurrency 1 and 16 the effect does not bite.

\paragraph{What this does and does not show.} It shows that an SLO computed
from stream-arrival gaps --- which is how load generators compute ITL ---
inverts the ranking under speculation at the concurrency we tested. It does
\emph{not} show that all reasonable service objectives do so. We measured
one workload, one arrival pattern (closed-loop fixed concurrency rather than
open-loop arrival rates), and four thresholds. An objective defined over
token-delivery curves, or over TTFT and end-to-end latency, would behave
differently, and we did not measure chunk-size distributions.

This is not an engine defect or a bug in our harness: a server cannot deliver
four tokens at four separate times when it produced them at one time. A
client measuring arrival gaps will see this wherever a decoding step emits
more than one token. At concurrency 1 the
effect is present but does not bite --- gaps are small in absolute terms and
speculative cells meet the same target 100\% of the time. It appears exactly
where serving operates.

We do not claim the quotient is the right metric. Dividing by
\tw{tokens\_per\_chunk} recovers an average and discards the burstiness an
ITL target exists to capture; whether a reader perceives four tokens every
94\,ms as smoother than one every 34\,ms is a question we did not study. Our
claim is narrower: \emph{ITL computed from stream arrivals does not measure
what its name says under speculation}, and on this stack, at this
concurrency, using it to gate a deployment inverts the decision. Whether a
given deployment's tooling computes it that way is a question for that
tooling. Prior work
(\S\ref{sec:related}) reports single-request latency, where the effect does
not bite --- consistent with it going unreported.

\section{Measurements after diagnosing four confounds}\label{sec:results}

\noindent
We say \emph{after diagnosing} rather than \emph{with all four controlled}.
Three are controlled: the attention backend is pinned, the execution path is
carried as an axis rather than fixed, and timing is reported as chunk
cadence. The fourth is not --- draft-side backend selection remains
uncontrolled (\S\ref{sec:c1}), because vLLM offers no way to pin it.

\subsection{The execution-path flag is not a free control}

We ran the full grid twice, 16 boots each, in independent containers, with
\tw{enforce\_eager} as an \emph{axis} rather than a pinned setting. Ratios
are graphs-off over graphs-on, so below 1.0 means eager is slower.

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\caption{Effect of \tw{-{}-enforce-eager}, two independent grids.}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Configuration & conc & grid 1 & grid 2\\
\midrule
none\,|\,bf16\,|\,auto & 1 & 0.95$\times$ & 0.96$\times$\\
none\,|\,bf16\,|\,auto & 64 & 0.96$\times$ & 0.96$\times$\\
none\,|\,\textbf{fp8}\,|\,auto & 1 & \textbf{0.49$\times$} & \textbf{0.68$\times$}\\
none\,|\,\textbf{fp8}\,|\,auto & 64 & \textbf{0.66$\times$} & \textbf{0.78$\times$}\\
none\,|\,\textbf{fp8}\,|\,fp8\_e4m3 & 1 & \textbf{0.50$\times$} & \textbf{0.66$\times$}\\
dflash\,|\,bf16\,|\,auto & 1 & 1.13$\times$ & 1.32$\times$\\
dflash\,|\,\textbf{fp8}\,|\,auto & 1 & \textbf{0.61$\times$} & \textbf{0.76$\times$}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent
\tw{-{}-enforce-eager} costs FP8-weight configurations a fifth to a half of
their throughput and costs BF16 essentially nothing. Individual boots do not
overlap on any FP8 row and overlap on every BF16 row, in both grids. $\tau$
is unmoved throughout.

The direction replicates on every cell; the coefficient does not, and that
is itself a result. The graphs-on arm reproduces everywhere
(0.95--1.06$\times$), the eager arm reproduces for BF16 (1.00--1.05$\times$)
and does not for FP8 weights (1.22--1.40$\times$). The eager+FP8 arm is
boot-unstable --- tight within a boot (20.2 and 20.5 tok/s on grid 1's two
repeats) and $1.4\times$ between them.

This study therefore found \emph{two} unstable dimensions, each with a stable
partner: speculation is unreproducible with CUDA graphs on and stable with them off;
FP8 weights are stable with graphs on and unstable with them off. Neither is
visible in a single boot, and neither predicts the other.

A third constraint on the same flag: it cannot boot speculative cells with
FP8 KV at all on this card, failing on an 892\,MiB float32 logits buffer
whose size is fixed by scheduler width times speculative width times
vocabulary. Had we pinned eager --- the obvious response to \S\ref{sec:c2}
--- every FP8-weight baseline would have been reduced, every speculative
FP8-KV cell would have failed to boot, and the speculation-versus-baseline
speedups would have been inflated on exactly the cells this paper is about,
with nothing in the output to indicate it.

\section{Threats to validity}\label{sec:threats}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{This is a single-configuration case study.} Every result is
one NVIDIA L4 (SM89), one target model (Qwen3-4B), one learned draft
checkpoint, one engine release (vLLM 0.29.0), one principal workload
(GSM8K), and one decoding configuration. Backend selection and CUDA-graph
behaviour all depend on engine version, kernels, GPU architecture and model,
so none of these findings should be assumed to transfer. What transfers is
the \emph{class} of measurement error, not the magnitudes.
\item \textbf{Boot and host effects are not separated.} Runs used rented
instances; we did not record GPU serial, clocks or power state, so a
host-to-host component cannot be distinguished from a boot-to-boot one in
\S\ref{sec:c2}.
\item \textbf{Configuration identity is not byte identity.} Our
\tw{cell\_id} hashes the declared configuration; model revisions are
declared as \tw{main}, a mutable ref, and dependencies as open ranges. We
resolved the commits post hoc and their upstream modification dates precede
every run, but the harness did not record them at launch. See
\tw{docs/reproducibility.md}.
\item \textbf{The mechanism is unidentified.} We show that the dispersion
is present with CUDA graphs enabled and collapses under eager execution. We
do not show which graph, scheduler, allocator, kernel or host property is
responsible, and we did not record GPU identity, so a host-to-host component
is not excluded.
\item \textbf{Mechanisms are not established} for the FP8-KV boot failure or
the FP8-weight eager penalty. Both are observed and reproduced; neither was
profiled. We state the readings we find plausible and mark them as readings.
\item \textbf{The draft-side backend confound is acknowledged, not removed.}
\item \textbf{Our retraction list covers errors we caught.} The same
blindness that produced them applies to finding them. We assume undiscovered
instances exist; one was found the same day we wrote the list.
\item \textbf{No base rate.} Comparable studies do not report their
retractions, so our count is evidence of legibility, not of unusual
error-proneness.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Every finding in the artifact carries a mandatory ``what this does not
establish'' section, and this paper inherits those limits rather than
softening them.

\noindent
None reports boot-level dispersion, and the single-request results in this
table are vulnerable to the confound in \S\ref{sec:c2}.

\textbf{On ``goodput''.} TurboSpec~\cite{liu2024turbospec} optimises a
quantity of that name in vLLM, so the word needs care. Theirs is the rate of
\emph{successfully generated (accepted) tokens}, used as a control signal;
it involves no latency target. Ours is the rate of requests \emph{meeting a
latency service objective}. The two are different measurements sharing a
name, and \S\ref{sec:c4} concerns only the latter.

Two relationships deserve stating precisely rather than by omission.

\textbf{Liu et al.}~\cite{liu2026illusion} report that acceptance varies
``within a request, across different requests, and across different
datasets''. That is variation of the \emph{quantity}; \S\ref{sec:c3} measures
the \emph{instrument} --- how much a repeated identical measurement moves.
The two are complementary, and conflating them would be a mistake in either
direction. They also establish that increasing batch size systematically
reduces SD's relative speedup, which is prior art for load-dependence and we
claim no novelty there.

\textbf{Kong et al.}~\cite{kong2026latency} validate a latency model on vLLM
SD measurements to $R^2 = 0.997$ using a single-sweep methodology with no
repeated runs and no discussion of CUDA graphs. Our \S\ref{sec:c2} identifies
an error term that such a protocol cannot see. We do not claim their results
are wrong --- they measure A100/H100 where we measure L4, and
\S\ref{sec:c2} localises the effect to draft-model speculation --- only that
the term is unmeasured and worth checking.

\textbf{On hardware.} Liu et al.\ use H100; Kong et al.\ use A100 and H100;
both use FP16 KV. The regime where KV capacity actually binds, and where FP8
KV therefore has something to buy, is the smaller card. That is why we
measure on L4, and it is the gap this paper occupies.

\section{Artifact}\label{sec:artifact}

Harness, raw records, derived tables and findings:
\url{https://github.com/akshathtiwari/spec-fp8-study}.

Two scripts are worth naming. \tw{analysis/check\_provenance.py} verifies
that every finding's citations resolve to stored records, that stored ids
still recompute from their stored configs, and that no result rests on
terminal output; it exits with the defect count. \tw{analysis/check\_paper.py}
verifies that every numeric figure in this paper appears in the underlying
records. Both run in \tw{paper/build.sh} before \LaTeX{} does, so a paper
that fails its own audit is not typeset, and both have found real defects in
this document --- including two tables that had silently diverged from the
findings they summarise.

At the time of writing \tw{check\_provenance.py} reports two defects, and we
state them rather than suppress them: findings F009 and F014 rest on probe
output that predates the harness's per-measurement recording, so their
numbers are not independently checkable from the repository. Neither is
cited in this paper. The remaining twenty-five findings resolve to stored
records.

\paragraph{What content-addressing does and does not prove.} A
\tw{cell\_id} guarantees two records came from the same \emph{declared}
configuration. It does not guarantee the same model bytes, engine build, or
dependency versions. \tw{argv\_fingerprint} and the per-record \tw{env}
block close part of the gap; \tw{docs/reproducibility.md} states the rest,
including the resolved model commits and the five changes that would make
the artifact airtight.

The entire study was run on rented per-second L4 time at a cost in the tens
of dollars. The error analysis in \S\ref{sec:c1}--\S\ref{sec:c4} is what that
budget can establish, and on the evidence of \S\ref{sec:related} it is what
the field is missing --- rather than another single-run speedup number
measured on hardware most readers cannot rent.
